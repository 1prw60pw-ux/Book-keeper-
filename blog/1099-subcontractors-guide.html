Do I Need to Send a 1099 to My Subcontractors?

By Patricia Woods · August 10, 2026

If you paid an independent contractor for business services and the total for the year crosses the IRS reporting threshold, you generally need to send them a 1099-NEC by the end of January. If you're not sure whether your situation qualifies, the short version is: when in doubt, it's safer to send one than to skip it.

Who actually counts as a subcontractor here?

This applies to people or businesses you paid for services, not products. Think web designers, freelance writers, cleaning crews, IT help, delivery drivers, consultants, or a handyman who fixed up your office. It does not apply to employees (they get a W-2) and it does not apply to purchases of physical goods or inventory.

It also generally doesn't apply if you paid the contractor through a credit card, debit card, or a third-party service like PayPal or Venmo's business platform. Those payments get reported by the payment processor instead, on a different form. This trips people up constantly, so it's worth double-checking how each payment was actually made.

Does it matter if they're an LLC or a corporation?

Usually you don't need to send a 1099 to a corporation, but LLCs are a gray area — some are taxed as corporations, some aren't. This is exactly why the W-9 form matters so much (more on that below). Don't guess based on the business name. "Smith Contracting LLC" could be taxed either way, and the W-9 tells you which.

What's the dollar threshold, and why won't this article name one?

There is a minimum amount you have to pay someone in a year before a 1099 is required, and it's been the same number for a long time. But reporting thresholds are exactly the kind of detail the IRS can change with little notice, and getting it wrong in either direction causes real problems — either you miss a required filing, or you send unnecessary paperwork to someone who didn't need it. Confirm the current threshold on IRS.gov or with your accountant before you finalize your list each year, rather than relying on last year's number from memory.

What happens if I get this wrong?

Two ways this goes badly:

  • You skip a 1099 you should have sent. The IRS can assess penalties per form, and the penalty amount typically increases the longer you wait to correct it. There's also a business tax angle — some tax preparers will ask whether you filed required 1099s before they'll sign off on deducting those contractor payments.
  • You send one late. Even if you eventually file it, missing the deadline can trigger a penalty. The deadline for giving contractors their copy and filing with the IRS is typically the end of January, which comes up faster than people expect right after the holidays.

Neither mistake is catastrophic if it's a one-time oversight and you correct it quickly, but it's the kind of thing that's genuinely painless to do right and annoying to fix after the fact.

What should I gather before I sit down to do this?

Get these ready before you try to file anything:

  • A completed Form W-9 from every contractor you paid — collect this before you pay them the first time, not in January when you're scrambling. It gives you their legal name, business type, and taxpayer ID number.
  • A running total of what you paid each contractor for the year, broken out by how you paid them (check, cash, direct deposit, credit card, PayPal, etc.).
  • Their current mailing address or email, since you're required to get them a copy of the form.
  • Your own business's EIN or taxpayer ID, since that goes on the form too.

What's the easiest way to not have to think about this every January?

Collect a W-9 from every contractor before you cut the first check. Not after. Not "I'll get to it." Before. It takes them two minutes to fill out, and it saves you from chasing someone down in January who has since moved, changed their business name, or stopped returning your emails.

If you use QuickBooks or similar software, mark contractors as 1099 vendors from the start and log payments consistently by method. That one habit does most of the sorting work for you automatically when it's time to file.

What if I've already fallen behind on this?

If you're realizing now that you should have sent 1099s in prior years and didn't, that's fixable — it's not the kind of thing to just quietly hope goes unnoticed. Late filing is generally treated better by the IRS than no filing at all. The sooner it's addressed, the fewer penalties tend to stack up.

Where this gets genuinely complicated

Mixed payment methods (part check, part PayPal) to the same contractor, contractors who are actually misclassified employees, and contractors who never gave you a W-9 are the three situations that cause the most headaches. None of them are unusual, and none of them require guessing your way through.
