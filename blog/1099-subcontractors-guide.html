Do I Need to Send a 1099 to My Subcontractors?

By Patricia Woods · August 10, 2026

If you paid an individual, partnership, or LLC $600 or more during the year for work they did for your business, you likely need to send them a 1099-NEC. The exceptions are payments to corporations (usually), payments made by credit card or through a platform like PayPal, and anything you paid an actual employee (that goes on a W-2 instead).

The rule sounds simple until you're staring at a list of everyone you paid last year trying to remember who was a contractor and who was a vendor. Here's how to sort it out.

Who actually counts as a "subcontractor" for this purpose?

Anyone who is not your employee but did work for your business — someone you didn't withhold taxes for, didn't provide benefits to, and who controls how and when they do the work. Common examples: a freelance graphic designer, a contract bookkeeper, a handyman you hired for a repair, a consultant, a delivery driver who isn't on payroll.

It doesn't matter if they only did one small job for you. If the total you paid them over the year crosses the threshold, the payment type matters more than the size of the relationship.

Does it matter how I paid them?

Yes, and this trips people up constantly. If you paid someone by cash, check, or direct bank transfer, that payment generally counts toward the 1099 threshold and you're responsible for reporting it.

If you paid them through a credit card, debit card, or a third-party platform like PayPal, Venmo (business transactions), or Stripe, you generally do not issue a 1099 for that payment — the payment processor is responsible for reporting it instead, typically on a 1099-K. This is one of the most commonly misunderstood parts of the rule, so if a big chunk of your contractor payments went through a platform, it's worth double-checking with your accountant which payments you're actually on the hook for.

What about LLCs and corporations?

Sole proprietors, individuals, and most partnerships and LLCs get a 1099 if they cross the threshold. Payments to a business taxed as a C-corp or S-corp are usually exempt — with a notable exception for payments to attorneys, which often need to be reported regardless of how the law firm is structured. This is exactly the kind of detail that's easy to get backwards. The safest move is to collect a completed Form W-9 from every contractor before you pay them anything. That form tells you their business structure and tax ID, so you're not guessing in January.

What happens if I don't send one?

The IRS can assess penalties for each 1099 you fail to file or file late, and the penalty amount increases the longer it goes unfiled and increases again if the IRS decides the failure was intentional. There's no exact dollar figure worth quoting here because penalty amounts are adjusted periodically — but the short version is that skipping a required 1099 is not a quiet mistake. It's also a red flag if that contractor gets audited, because their reported income won't match what the IRS was expecting to see from you.

Beyond the penalty, there's a practical cost: if you didn't collect a W-9 up front and now need someone's tax ID number in January, you may find they've stopped responding to your emails.

What to gather now, before it's a scramble

  • A signed W-9 from every contractor you paid, collected before or at the time of first payment, not after.
  • A running total of what you paid each contractor, broken out by payment method — cash/check/transfer versus card/platform.
  • Their correct legal name and business structure, taken from the W-9, not from memory or an invoice header.
  • Confirmation of the current dollar threshold and filing deadline directly from the IRS or your accountant — these details are stable most years but do get revisited, and starting the year with outdated numbers is an easy way to miss a deadline.

When do these need to go out?

1099-NEC forms are generally due to both the contractor and the IRS in late January, which is earlier than most other tax deadlines and catches people off guard. Because the exact date can shift slightly depending on weekends and holidays, confirm the current-year deadline rather than assuming it matches last year.

A simple way to stay ahead of this next year

Collect the W-9 before the first payment goes out — not when you're rushing to file in January. Keep a simple spreadsheet or use your bookkeeping software to tag every contractor payment as it happens, noting the payment method. That habit alone eliminates most of the year-end scramble.
