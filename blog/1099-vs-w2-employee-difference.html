1099 Contractor or W-2 Employee? How to Tell the Difference

By Patricia Woods · September 15, 2026

The difference comes down to control, not what you call the person or what you both agreed to on paper. If you control how, when, and where someone does their work and provide their tools, they're probably an employee. If they run their own business, set their own methods, and could do the same work for someone else tomorrow, they're probably a contractor.

That sounds simple until you're staring at an actual person doing actual work for you. Here's how to think it through.

What does the IRS actually look at?

The IRS uses a common-law test built around three broad categories. No single factor decides it — they weigh the whole picture.

  • Behavioral control: Do you tell them how to do the job, set their hours, require specific training, or supervise the details? Employees get instructions. Contractors get outcomes.
  • Financial control: Do they have their own equipment, cover their own expenses, invoice you, and have the chance to make a profit or take a loss? Contractors usually invest in their own business and can work for multiple clients.
  • Relationship of the parties: Is there a written contract? Do they get benefits like paid time off or health insurance? Is the work ongoing and central to your business, or project-based and separate from your core operations?

A worker doesn't need to fail all three categories to be misclassified. Even one strong factor pointing toward "employee" — like you dictating their daily schedule and methods — can be enough for a determination to go against you.

What questions should you ask yourself?

Walk through these honestly:

  • Do I set their work hours, or do they choose when to get it done?
  • Do I provide the equipment, software, or workspace they use?
  • Could they realistically do this same kind of work for another company right now?
  • Do I pay them a flat salary or hourly wage, or do they invoice me for completed work?
  • Is what they do central to my business (like a full-time salesperson) or a specialized outside service (like a graphic designer for one project)?
  • Have I asked them to sign an exclusivity agreement, or wear a uniform, or attend regular staff meetings?

If most answers point to control and integration, you're likely looking at an employee, no matter what the contract says.

What does it cost to get this wrong?

Misclassifying an employee as a contractor is one of the more expensive mistakes a small business can make, because it usually isn't caught until years later, when it's already compounded.

If the IRS or a state agency reclassifies a worker, you can end up owing back payroll taxes for the period they worked, plus penalties and interest on top of that. Some states also have their own labor departments that pursue unpaid unemployment insurance and workers' comp premiums separately from the IRS. The exact rates and penalty structures change and depend on your situation, so don't rely on a number you saw somewhere online — confirm current figures with the IRS or your accountant before you make a decision either way.

There's also a real cost on the other side: treating an actual employee as a 1099 contractor denies them protections they're legally entitled to, and that can turn into a wage claim or a lawsuit, separate from any tax issue.

What if I'm still not sure?

You're allowed to ask the IRS directly. There's a form specifically for this — you or the worker can file it to request an official determination on a specific working relationship. It takes time to get an answer, so it's not a fast fix if you need clarity right now, but it's the most reliable answer you can get in writing.

In the meantime, the safer move when you're genuinely unsure is to lean toward W-2. It costs more upfront in payroll taxes, but it's far cheaper than a retroactive reclassification.

What to gather before you decide

If you're evaluating an existing worker or setting up a new one, pull together:

  • Any written agreement or contract you have with them
  • A rough log of their actual hours and how much say they have over their schedule
  • A list of who owns the equipment, software licenses, and tools they use
  • Whether they invoice you or you run them through payroll
  • Whether they work for other clients besides you
  • How central their role is to your day-to-day operations

Having this documented protects you either way — it shows you made a reasoned decision based on the actual working relationship, not just a preference for avoiding payroll taxes.

Where this fits with your bookkeeping

Worker classification isn't just an HR question — it shows up in your books as payroll versus contractor payments, and it affects what forms you issue at year-end. Getting it right from the start saves you a much messier cleanup later.
