Cash vs Accrual Accounting: Which Should Your Business Use?

By Patricia Woods · September 1, 2026

Most small businesses should start with cash basis accounting because it's simpler and matches how money actually moves in and out of your bank account. But if you carry inventory, extend credit to customers, or your business has grown past a certain size, accrual basis may be required or simply give you a truer picture of how you're doing. Here's how to tell which one fits you.

What's the actual difference?

Cash basis: you record income when you receive the money and expenses when you pay the bill. If a customer hasn't paid you yet, it doesn't show up as income. If you haven't paid a vendor yet, it's not an expense yet.

Accrual basis: you record income when you earn it (the invoice goes out or the work is done) and expenses when you incur them (the bill arrives), regardless of when cash actually changes hands.

Same business, same month, two different-looking bank balances and two different-looking profit numbers. Neither one is "wrong" — they answer different questions.

Why would a small business pick cash basis?

It's easier to understand at a glance: your books track your bank account. You're not taxed on money you haven't collected yet, which matters a lot if a client is slow to pay. Most solo owners, freelancers, and small service-based businesses use cash basis because it's less bookkeeping work and it's easier to explain to yourself at 11pm when you're trying to figure out if you can make payroll.

Why would a small business need accrual basis?

Accrual gives you a more accurate picture of profitability over time, especially if:

  • You carry inventory and need to match the cost of goods sold to the revenue they generated
  • You regularly invoice customers and wait weeks or months to get paid
  • You have outside investors, a bank loan, or a lender who wants financials that follow standard accounting practice
  • Your revenue has grown to a size where the IRS requires it — there are gross receipts thresholds and rules around inventory that can make cash basis no longer an option for your business

That last point matters. There are real IRS rules about which businesses are allowed to use cash basis, tied to revenue size and whether you sell inventory. Those thresholds change and have exceptions, so don't rely on a number you saw in a blog post from a few years ago — confirm your specific situation with the IRS or your accountant before you decide.

Can you switch later?

Yes, but it's not as simple as flipping a switch in QuickBooks. Changing your accounting method for tax purposes generally requires filing a specific request with the IRS and can affect how certain income and expenses are treated in the year of the change. It's worth doing right the first time, and worth getting help if you need to change later — this is one of those areas where a small mistake can mean redoing months of books.

What to check before you decide

Gather these before you make the call, or before a conversation with your bookkeeper or accountant:

  • Your total revenue for the last one to three years
  • Whether you hold inventory, and roughly how much it's worth at any given time
  • Whether you invoice clients and let them pay later, versus getting paid at time of sale
  • Whether you have or plan to seek a business loan or outside investment
  • Your current QuickBooks or accounting software setup, and which method it's currently tracking

Getting this wrong doesn't just create confusing reports — it can mean your tax return doesn't match your actual financial activity, which raises questions if the IRS ever looks closely, and it can mean you're paying tax on income you haven't collected yet or missing deductions you're entitled to. It's also just harder to run a business when your books don't tell you the truth about your cash position.

What most small businesses actually do

In practice, a lot of small service businesses keep cash basis books for day-to-day decision-making because it's simpler and matches the bank account, while working with a bookkeeper who keeps an eye on whether accrual adjustments are needed for tax filing or for loan applications. You don't have to pick one method forever and never revisit it — you just need to know which one you're using right now and why.
