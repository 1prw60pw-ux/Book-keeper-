Do I Need to Send a 1099 to My Subcontractors?

By Patricia Woods · August 10, 2026

Do I need to send a 1099?

If you paid an individual, partnership, or LLC (not taxed as a corporation) $600 or more during the year for services related to your business, the answer is almost always yes. This applies to subcontractors, freelancers, cleaners, consultants, and similar non-employee help. It does not usually apply to payments to corporations or to purchases of goods rather than services.

That $600 figure has been stable for a long time, but thresholds and rules can shift, so confirm the current number with the IRS or your accountant before you assume it still applies exactly as described here.

What counts as a "subcontractor" for this purpose?

Basically anyone you paid to do work for your business who is not on your payroll. Think: a freelance graphic designer, a subcontracted electrician, a virtual assistant, a bookkeeper you hired for a one-time project. If you controlled how the work got done and paid them as an employee, that's payroll, not a 1099 situation — those are two very different tax treatments and mixing them up can create real problems.

What if I paid them through a credit card or PayPal/Venmo?

If you paid electronically through a third-party payment network, the payment processor may be the one responsible for reporting that income, not you. This is a common point of confusion. Ask your accountant how your specific payment method affects your reporting obligation — it can change what you're required to file.

How do I know if they're a corporation?

You ask them — before you pay them, ideally. This is exactly what a Form W-9 is for. Have every contractor fill one out before their first payment goes out. It tells you their business structure, their tax ID number, and their legal name. Without it, you're guessing, and guessing wrong means either filing when you didn't need to or — worse — not filing when you did.

What do I actually need to gather?

  • A completed Form W-9 from every contractor, collected before you pay them, not in January when you're scrambling
  • Their legal business name and tax ID number (SSN or EIN) exactly as it appears on the W-9
  • Total amount paid to them during the calendar year, added up from your bookkeeping records
  • Their current mailing address
  • Confirmation of whether they're a corporation, sole proprietor, partnership, or LLC — and if LLC, how it's taxed

If your bookkeeping is organized throughout the year, this is a ten-minute task in January. If it's not, it's a multi-day scramble through bank statements and email threads. This is one of the biggest reasons small business owners fall behind on 1099s — not because the rule is complicated, but because the records weren't kept as the year went along.

What does it cost me to get this wrong?

The IRS can assess penalties for filing 1099s late, filing them with incorrect information, or not filing them at all. Penalty amounts vary depending on how late you are and whether the failure looks intentional, and they can add up per form if you have several contractors. There's also a practical cost: if you're ever audited, your contractor payments may not hold up as legitimate business deductions without proper documentation. Ask your accountant for current penalty amounts — they do get adjusted periodically.

When are 1099s due?

1099-NEC forms are generally due to both the contractor and the IRS by the end of January following the tax year. Because deadlines and any related grace periods can shift slightly year to year, confirm the exact date with the IRS or your accountant well before the end of the year — not after the holidays, when it's harder to reach anyone and easier to miss the date.

What's the simplest way to stay on top of this?

Three habits solve most of the stress:

  • Get a W-9 from every contractor before you send the first payment, no exceptions
  • Track contractor payments separately in your bookkeeping software all year, not just at tax time
  • Do a mid-year check-in (say, every summer) to see who's approaching or has crossed the threshold, so nobody surprises you in December

If you're using QuickBooks or similar software, most versions can flag 1099-eligible vendors automatically once they're set up correctly — but that setup has to happen before the payments, not after.

What if I'm behind on this already?

You're not the first person to realize in January that a folder of W-9s doesn't exist. It's fixable. The sooner you start gathering information from contractors, the more likely you are to file on time or close to it, which matters for minimizing any penalty.
