How Long Do I Have to Keep Business Receipts?

By Patricia Woods · August 21, 2026

For most business records, three years from the date you filed the related tax return is the standard rule of thumb. But some records need to stick around much longer, and a few — like anything tied to property you still own — should be kept until well after you sell or dispose of the asset.

What's the general three-year rule based on?

The IRS generally has three years from when you file a return to audit it or for you to file an amended return claiming a refund. That's why three years is the baseline most bookkeepers use for everyday records: receipts, invoices, bank statements, mileage logs, and anything that supports the numbers on a filed return.

So if you filed your 2023 tax return in April 2024, the safe rule is to keep the supporting records for that return until at least three years after the filing date — not three years from the end of the tax year. That distinction trips people up more than it should.

When do you need to keep records longer than three years?

A few situations stretch that window:

  • If you underreported income by a significant amount — the IRS gets a longer lookback period in cases of substantial underreporting. The exact percentage threshold and extended timeframe are worth confirming directly with the IRS or your accountant, since this isn't a situation you want to guess on.
  • If you didn't file a return at all, or filed a fraudulent one — there's effectively no time limit. Keep those records indefinitely.
  • Employment tax records — payroll records generally need to be kept for a period beyond the standard three years. If you run payroll, even for one employee, treat this as a separate retention category and check current guidance rather than assuming the general rule applies.
  • Bad debt or worthless securities — these have their own longer retention window.

None of these are places to guess. If any apply to you, it's worth a quick confirmation with your accountant on the current specifics rather than relying on memory or an old blog post — including this one.

What about equipment, property, or anything you depreciate?

This is the one people forget most often. If you bought a vehicle, computer, machinery, or property for the business, you need to keep the purchase records for as long as you own the asset — plus the standard retention period after you sell or dispose of it.

Why? Because those records establish your basis in the asset. If you toss the purchase receipt five years in because "it's been three years," you'll have no way to correctly calculate gain, loss, or depreciation when you eventually sell. This applies to real estate especially — keep those records for as long as you own the property and for years afterward.

What should you actually gather and hold onto?

In practice, "records" means more than receipts. Keep:

  • Bank and credit card statements for business accounts
  • Invoices you sent and received
  • Receipts for anything you're deducting, especially meals, travel, and equipment
  • Payroll records, including hours, pay rates, and tax deposits
  • Copies of filed tax returns themselves — permanently, ideally, since they're small and easy to store
  • Purchase and sale documents for any business property or equipment
  • Loan documents and lease agreements for the life of the agreement plus a few years after

Digital copies are fine. The IRS accepts scanned or photographed receipts as long as they're legible and complete. A shoebox of faded thermal-paper receipts from three years ago is often worse than a clear photo taken the day of purchase — those receipts fade to blank paper faster than people expect.

What does it actually cost you to get this wrong?

Two different problems show up here, and they're not the same:

Keeping too little. If you're audited and can't produce support for a deduction, the IRS can disallow it — meaning you owe the tax, plus interest, and possibly penalties. This is the expensive direction to get wrong, and it's the one small business owners hit most often, usually with mileage logs and meal receipts.

Keeping too little on property. If you can't document what you paid for an asset, you may end up overpaying tax on the sale because you can't prove your basis — the IRS will often assume it's zero, which means the entire sale price looks like taxable gain.

Keeping everything forever, disorganized. This isn't a legal risk, but it's a practical one — when tax season or an audit request comes, you're digging through years of clutter instead of pulling exactly what's needed. A simple year-by-year folder system, digital or physical, solves this without much effort.

A simple way to think about it

Three years for most day-to-day records. Longer — sometimes much longer — for payroll, unfiled or fraudulent returns, and anything tied to property you still own. When in doubt about a specific number or deadline that matters for your situation, it's worth a quick check with the IRS directly or with your accountant before you shred anything.
