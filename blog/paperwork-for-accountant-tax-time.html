What Paperwork Do I Need to Give My Accountant at Tax Time?

By Patricia Woods · November 3, 2026

The short answer

Your accountant needs anything that shows money coming in, money going out, and money you already paid toward taxes. For most small business owners, that breaks down into income records, expense records, payroll documents, and last year's return. The exact mix depends on your business, but if you gather these categories before your appointment, you'll save time and probably money.

What counts as income paperwork?

This includes any 1099 forms you received (1099-NEC, 1099-MISC, 1099-K, or others, depending on how clients or platforms paid you), bank statements for all business accounts, and a summary of total sales if you invoice clients directly. If you sell through a platform like Square, Stripe, Shopify, or a marketplace app, pull the annual summary report from that platform too. If you're not sure whether a form you received is one your accountant needs, bring it anyway. It's easier to set something aside than to track it down later.

What counts as expense paperwork?

Bring receipts or statements for anything you spent money on to run the business: supplies, software subscriptions, advertising, rent, utilities, insurance, vehicle mileage, travel, and professional fees. If you use a bookkeeping service or software like QuickBooks, a year-end profit and loss statement and balance sheet usually covers most of this in one document. If you don't have clean books, bring bank and credit card statements for every account used in the business, even personal accounts if you ever paid a business expense from them.

Do I need payroll records?

Yes, if you have employees or pay yourself through payroll. Gather your quarterly payroll tax filings, year-end wage summaries, and copies of any W-2s or 1099s you issued to others. If a payroll service handles this for you, they usually provide a year-end package. Make sure it's the final, corrected version, not a draft.

What about last year's return?

Always bring a copy of your prior year's tax return, even if the same person prepared it. It has carryover information your accountant needs, like depreciation schedules, loss carryforwards, or estimated payments already made. If you switched accountants this year, this one document saves the most back-and-forth.

What if I'm missing something?

Tell your accountant what you don't have instead of guessing or leaving it out. A missing 1099 or an incomplete expense category can usually be worked around, but a return filed with wrong or incomplete numbers can't always be fixed without amending it later, which costs more time and sometimes more money than getting it right the first time.

A practical checklist to pull together

  • Last year's tax return (business and personal, if they're connected)
  • All 1099s, W-2s, and 1098s you received
  • Profit and loss statement and balance sheet, or bank/credit card statements if you don't have these
  • Payroll tax filings and year-end wage reports, if you have employees
  • Receipts or logs for vehicle use, home office, and large purchases
  • Any letters from the IRS or state tax agency received during the year
  • Estimated tax payments made during the year, with dates and amounts

What does it actually cost to get this wrong?

Missing income documents can lead to an IRS notice later, because the IRS often receives a copy of your 1099s whether or not you do. Missing expense records mean you may pay tax on income you didn't really keep, simply because there's no proof of the deduction. And incomplete payroll records can create mismatches that take months to untangle. None of this is usually disastrous, but it's almost always more expensive and more stressful to fix after the fact than to hand over complete paperwork up front.

A note on specifics

Thresholds for which 1099 you receive, what counts as deductible, and current filing deadlines change from year to year. This article describes the categories of paperwork in general terms. For the current rules that apply to your specific situation, confirm with the IRS or your tax preparer before you file.
