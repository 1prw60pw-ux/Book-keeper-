Do I Need to Pay Quarterly Estimated Taxes?

By Patricia Woods · October 13, 2026

If you expect to owe more than a small amount of tax for the year and no one is withholding tax from your income, the answer is probably yes. This mainly applies to self-employed people, freelancers, landlords, and small business owners who don't have an employer withholding taxes from a paycheck.

The IRS doesn't want to wait until April for its money. If you're earning income without withholding, it expects you to pay tax on that income roughly as you earn it, in four installments throughout the year.

Who actually has to pay these?

You likely need to make quarterly payments if most or all of these are true:

  • You're self-employed, a freelancer, or run a sole proprietorship, partnership, or S-corp and take owner's draws instead of a W-2 paycheck
  • You have significant income from rental property, investments, or a side business
  • No employer is withholding federal tax for you, or withholding alone won't cover what you'll owe
  • You expect to owe a meaningful amount of tax when you file your return, not just get a refund

If you have a regular job with a W-2 and only a small amount of side income, you may be able to cover everything just by adjusting your W-2 withholding instead of filing quarterly payments. That's worth asking your accountant about — it's often simpler.

How do I know if I owe enough to matter?

The IRS has a specific dollar threshold below which you don't need to bother with quarterly payments at all, and "safe harbor" rules that protect you from penalties if you pay a certain percentage of last year's tax bill or this year's. These numbers are set by the IRS and can shift, so rather than guess at them here, check the current figures directly on IRS.gov or confirm them with your accountant before you decide to skip a payment.

What matters more than memorizing the exact threshold is the habit: if you're earning untaxed income regularly, assume you owe something and set money aside for it until you or your bookkeeper confirms otherwise.

When are the payments due?

Estimated taxes are paid in four installments spread across the year, not quarterly in the calendar sense — the due dates don't fall exactly three months apart. The IRS publishes the exact due dates each year, and they can shift slightly if a date lands on a weekend or holiday. Don't rely on memory from a prior year; check the current year's schedule.

How do I figure out how much to pay?

A reasonable starting point is this: look at what you actually owed in tax last year, or project what you'll owe this year based on your income so far, and divide that estimate across your remaining payments. If your income is uneven — common for seasonal businesses or new freelancers — you may need to adjust each quarter rather than paying the same amount four times.

This is one of the areas where doing it yourself with a rough guess can backfire. Pay too little and you risk an underpayment penalty. Pay too much and you've handed the IRS an interest-free loan you didn't need to give them.

What does it cost me to get this wrong?

Two mistakes happen most often:

  • Not paying at all because no one told you that you needed to. This usually results in an underpayment penalty plus interest, calculated from each missed due date, not just from April.
  • Underestimating your income mid-year — a good quarter comes in, you don't adjust your next payment, and you end up short by the time you file.

Neither is catastrophic on its own, but the penalties compound the longer they go unaddressed, and they're entirely avoidable with a little planning.

What should I have ready before I calculate this?

Gather these before you or your bookkeeper run the numbers:

  • Last year's tax return, especially the total tax owed
  • Year-to-date profit and loss for your business or self-employment income
  • Any 1099s or other income documentation received so far this year
  • A record of any estimated payments you've already made this year
  • An estimate of major upcoming expenses or income changes for the rest of the year

With those in hand, calculating a reasonable quarterly payment usually takes less time than people expect — the hard part is just remembering to do it on schedule.

What if I've already missed a payment?

Make the next one as soon as you can and don't skip the rest waiting for "next year" to start fresh. Catching up partially still reduces the penalty and interest compared to doing nothing. If you're not sure how much is owed at this point, that's a good moment to get a second set of eyes on your books.
