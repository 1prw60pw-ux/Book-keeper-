Which of My Business Expenses Are Actually Tax Deductible?

By Patricia Woods · September 8, 2026

A business expense is deductible if it's both ordinary and necessary for running your business — meaning it's common in your line of work and helpful for earning income. It also has to be a real business cost, not a personal one you're hoping to sneak through. Beyond that, the details matter a lot, and this is where most owners either overpay the IRS or set themselves up for an uncomfortable audit conversation.

What does "ordinary and necessary" actually mean?

"Ordinary" doesn't mean you buy it every day — it means other people in your industry would recognize it as a normal cost of doing that kind of business. "Necessary" doesn't mean you couldn't survive without it; it means it's appropriate and helpful for your work. A graphic designer buying a new monitor is ordinary and necessary. A graphic designer buying a boat is not, even if she uses it to "meet clients."

The test isn't whether an expense feels justified to you. It's whether it would hold up if someone outside your business looked at it and asked, "how does this help you make money?"

What kinds of expenses usually qualify?

Most everyday operating costs are deductible when they're genuinely tied to the business, including things like:

  • Rent or a portion of your home used regularly and exclusively for business
  • Software subscriptions, business insurance, and professional fees
  • Advertising, website costs, and business cards
  • Supplies and equipment used for the work
  • Vehicle costs for business driving, tracked properly
  • Wages and contractor payments
  • Business-related education and licensing

The specific rules, limits, and percentages for things like home office space, vehicle mileage, and meals change from year to year and depend on your situation. Don't rely on last year's numbers or something you read on a forum — confirm the current figures with the IRS or your accountant before you file.

Where do people usually get this wrong?

Three areas cause the most trouble:

Mixing personal and business

If you use one credit card for both your business and your personal life, you're making your own bookkeeping harder and your deductions harder to defend. A meal with a friend that turns into a work conversation is not automatically a business expense. The IRS looks at intent and documentation, not vibes.

Meals and entertainment

Business meals can often be partially deductible, but entertainment expenses — think concert tickets or a round of golf — are generally treated differently and more restrictively. The rules here have shifted over the years, so don't assume the old percentages still apply.
