What Does a Bookkeeper Cost, and When Is One Worth It?

By Patricia Woods · September 22, 2026

A bookkeeper typically costs anywhere from a few hundred to over a thousand dollars a month, depending on how messy your books are, how many transactions you have, and whether you also need payroll or reporting. It's worth hiring one the moment you're spending more hours untangling your finances than growing your business, or the moment a mistake in your books could actually cost you money.

What actually drives the price?

Bookkeepers don't charge a flat universal rate, because no two businesses generate the same amount of work. The main things that move the price up or down are:

  • Transaction volume. A business with 20 bank transactions a month costs less to keep clean than one with 400.
  • Number of accounts. Multiple bank accounts, credit cards, and payment processors (Stripe, PayPal, Square) all need to be reconciled separately.
  • Payroll. Running payroll for even one or two employees adds real work and compliance risk, so it's usually priced separately from basic bookkeeping.
  • State of your records. Clean, current books cost less to maintain than a shoebox of receipts from eight months ago.
  • What you actually want. Simple categorization and reconciliation costs less than monthly financial statements, cash flow reports, or year-round tax planning support.

Two businesses that look similar from the outside can have very different bookkeeping costs, because the real driver is volume and complexity, not revenue.

Hourly, flat monthly fee, or something else?

Most independent bookkeepers price their work one of two ways:

  • Hourly rates work well for one-time projects, like a catch-up cleanup or a QuickBooks setup, where the total hours are hard to predict up front.
  • Flat monthly fees are more common for ongoing bookkeeping, once a bookkeeper has looked at your books and knows roughly what the monthly workload will be. This gives you a predictable bill instead of a surprise invoice.

Be cautious of any quote given before the bookkeeper has actually looked at your bank feeds or prior books. A real quote should come after a short review, not before.

When is it actually worth paying for one?

There's no universal revenue number where "you need a bookkeeper" kicks in. It's more about symptoms. Consider it worth the cost if:

  • You're not sure if you made or lost money last month.
  • You're guessing at numbers when you file estimated taxes.
  • Invoices go out late, or you're not sure who still owes you money.
  • You've gotten a notice from the IRS or your state and didn't fully understand why.
  • You're spending evenings or weekends on reconciliations instead of on the work that actually makes you money.
  • You're about to apply for a loan or bring on an investor and need clean financials fast.

If none of that sounds like you yet, a spreadsheet and some discipline might genuinely be enough for now. Bookkeeping help is worth the most when your time has a clear dollar value elsewhere, or when errors in your books have real consequences.

What does it actually cost you to skip it or do it wrong?

This is the part that doesn't show up on an invoice but matters more than the monthly fee:

  • Missed deductions. Expenses that never get categorized properly often just get lost, which means you pay more in taxes than you had to.
  • Penalties and interest. Late or inaccurate payroll tax deposits and filings can trigger penalties. The rules and amounts here change, so don't rely on a rough memory of what you heard once — confirm current penalty structures with the IRS or a tax professional if this applies to you.
  • A bigger cleanup bill later. A year of disorganized transactions is far more expensive to sort out after the fact than it would have been to keep current each month. Catch-up work is almost always priced higher than ongoing maintenance, hour for hour.
  • Bad decisions from bad numbers. If you don't actually know your profit margin or cash position, you can end up overspending, underpricing, or hiring before you can afford to.

What to gather before you talk to a bookkeeper

If you're getting a quote, having these ready will make the number more accurate and save you time:

  • Bank and credit card statements for the last 3–12 months
  • Access to (or a description of) your current accounting software, if any
  • A rough count of monthly transactions across all accounts
  • Whether you run payroll, and for how many people
  • Any outstanding notices from the IRS or state tax agency
  • Your last filed tax return, if you have one

A bookkeeper who's worth hiring should be able to look at this and tell you plainly what it will cost and why — not just quote a number pulled from a general price list.

The short version

A bookkeeper is worth the cost when the time, stress, or risk of doing it yourself is worth more than the fee. For a lot of small business owners, that point arrives quietly — not with one big crisis, but with a slow accumulation of unopened statements and a growing sense that nobody, including them, actually knows the numbers.
