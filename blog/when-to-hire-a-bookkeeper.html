When Should I Hire a Bookkeeper Instead of Doing It Myself?

By Patricia Woods · October 27, 2026

Hire a bookkeeper when the time you spend on your books starts costing you more than it would cost to pay someone else to do it right — or when you genuinely don't know if your numbers are accurate anymore. If either of those is true, you're already past the point where DIY is saving you money.

What are the signs you've outgrown DIY bookkeeping?

A few patterns show up again and again with business owners who finally make the switch:

  • You're doing your books on weekends or late at night because there's no time for it during the week.
  • You're not sure if a transaction is business or personal, and you've started guessing.
  • Your bank balance and your bookkeeping software disagree, and you don't know why.
  • You dread tax season because you know your records are a mess underneath.
  • You've hired your first employee or contractor and payroll suddenly has rules you didn't know existed.
  • You want a loan, a line of credit, or an investor, and you realize your financials don't look professional enough to hand over.

Any one of these on its own might not mean much. Two or three together usually mean it's time.

Is it really about the money, or about your time?

Both, but time usually comes first. If you're spending several hours a week wrestling with categorizing transactions, reconciling accounts, or figuring out why a report looks wrong, that's time you're not spending on clients, sales, or your actual craft. Put a number on what your hour is worth in your business, multiply it by the hours you're losing to bookkeeping, and compare that to what a bookkeeper would charge. For most small business owners, that math flips faster than they expect.

The money side shows up later, usually at tax time or during a cash crunch — when bad records mean you either overpay, underpay, or can't tell your accountant what actually happened in your business last year.

What does it actually cost you to wait too long?

This is where DIY bookkeeping gets expensive in ways that don't show up right away:

  • Missed deductions. If expenses aren't categorized correctly, you may be overpaying your taxes without knowing it.
  • Payroll mistakes. Misclassifying a worker or missing a filing deadline can trigger penalties and interest — the specific amounts depend on the situation, so this isn't something to guess your way through.
  • A bigger cleanup bill later. A year of messy books is far more expensive to untangle than a year of clean ones. Catch-up work takes longer, and longer means it costs more.
  • Bad decisions made on bad numbers. If you don't trust your financials, you can't trust the decisions you're making based on them — pricing, hiring, spending.

None of this means you're doing something wrong by handling your own books. It means bookkeeping is its own skill, and at some point most growing businesses need more hours and more expertise than the owner has to give it.

What should you gather before you call a bookkeeper?

If you're ready to look into it, having this on hand makes the first conversation faster and more useful:

  • Bank and credit card statements for the past year, or as far back as your records go.
  • Access to whatever accounting software you're currently using, even if it's just a spreadsheet.
  • Your most recent tax return, if you have one for the business.
  • A list of any loans, lines of credit, or recurring payments tied to the business.
  • Payroll records, if you have employees or contractors.
  • A rough sense of how far behind you think your books are — even "I honestly don't know" is useful information.

You don't need everything perfectly organized. Part of what a bookkeeper does is make sense of what you already have, even if it's scattered across three apps and a shoebox of receipts.

What if you're not sure yet?

You don't have to be fully behind or in crisis mode to ask for help. A lot of business owners reach out just to get a second opinion on whether their current setup makes sense, or to have someone look at their books once and tell them honestly whether anything needs fixing. That conversation costs you nothing but a little time, and it usually answers the question for you one way or the other.
